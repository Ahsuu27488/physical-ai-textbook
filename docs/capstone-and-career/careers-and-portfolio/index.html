Careers and portfolio in physical AI

Beginner35 minBefore this:Finished or planned capstone

You have spent weeks building things that move. Now someone asks, "So what do you do?" and the honest answer is a pile of repositories, half-remembered debugging stories and a robot that once fetched a red cup. This lesson turns that pile into a story a hiring manager, a lab or a collaborator can understand in two minutes.

You will be able to

  • Describe the main roles in robotics and which skills each one leans on.
  • Turn the capstone and lab work into a portfolio with repositories, videos and write-ups.
  • Start contributing to open-source robotics projects.
  • Prepare for robotics interviews and follow a 90-day plan after the course.

The roles​

"Robotics engineer" covers very different jobs. Titles vary by company, but most work falls into a few families.

  • Robotics software engineer. Builds the system that runs on the robot: ROS 2 nodes, state machines, drivers, tooling, tests, deployment. The most common entry point.
  • Perception engineer. Turns sensor data into understanding: detection, segmentation, depth, tracking, SLAM, calibration.
  • Controls engineer. Makes the robot move accurately and stably: kinematics, dynamics, feedback control, trajectory generation, whole-body control for legged machines.
  • Machine learning and robot learning engineer. Trains policies and models: imitation learning, reinforcement learning, datasets, evaluation, foundation models for robots.
  • Simulation engineer. Builds simulated worlds, sensors and tools for testing and training: Gazebo, Isaac Sim, synthetic data and continuous integration.
  • Field and applications engineer. Deploys robots with customers, tunes them in real environments, and brings back what breaks.

Other doors exist: mechanical and electrical design, hardware bring-up, safety and test engineering, product and program roles. Even if you do not choose them, working alongside them is part of every robotics job.

Be T-shaped

Go deep in one area and be able to work across the stack. A perception specialist who can launch a ROS 2 system, read a transform tree and deploy to hardware is far more useful than one who cannot. The capstone gave you the breadth; your portfolio should show one place where you went deep.

Building your portfolio​

A portfolio is evidence. It answers: can this person build something real, explain it and finish it? Aim for three to five strong pieces rather than twenty weak ones.

1. The capstone, done properly​

This is your flagship. Make the repository excellent:

  • A README that says what the project does in the first two sentences, shows a GIF or screenshot, lists requirements and gives a one-command quick start.
  • A short architecture diagram (reuse the node graph).
  • Your evaluation results: success rate, failure breakdown and what you improved because of it.
  • Clean commit history, tests that pass and a license.

2. A demo video​

Three minutes maximum. Show the robot doing the task, then show one failure and how you handled it. Failures shown honestly build more trust than polished perfection. Put a caption on every scene so it works without sound.

3. A write-up​

One page or a blog post about a hard problem you solved: what failed, how you diagnosed it, what you changed and what you measured. This is the artifact that interviewers actually discuss.

4. A depth project​

Choose the area you want to be hired for and build something focused. Examples: a better detector for transparent cups with a measured improvement; a Kalman filter comparison on real recorded data; a reproducible imitation learning pipeline with a small dataset and clear evaluation; a Nav2 plugin or custom behavior tree node; a simulation environment others can reuse.

5. Something that runs on hardware​

Even a small robot kit, a servo arm or a rover with a camera. Real hardware shows you can handle the mess that simulation hides. See Deploying on hardware.

Here is a README skeleton that works well.

README.md
# Project name: one sentence of what it does

![demo](docs/demo.gif)

## What it does
Two or three sentences in plain words.

## Results
| Metric | Value |
|---|---|
| Success rate (20 trials) | 70% |
| Median task time | 94 s |
| Biggest failure stage | grasp (40% of failures) |

## Architecture
Link to a node graph image and a short description of each package.

## Quick start
    git clone ...
    ./scripts/bringup.sh
    ./scripts/evaluate.sh

## Limitations and next steps
What does not work yet, and what you would do next.

Quote only numbers your logs support. Hiring managers read hundreds of project pages and a made-up metric is easy to spot in a conversation.

Open-source contribution​

Robotics is built on open source: ROS 2, Nav2, MoveIt 2, Gazebo and countless drivers and tools. Contributing is one of the best ways to learn and to be seen.

  1. Start with documentation and small fixes. A typo, an unclear tutorial step or a broken link is a real contribution and teaches you the review process.
  2. Reproduce and report bugs well. A bug report with a minimal example and your versions is valuable. Maintainers are often short of exactly this.
  3. Pick up labelled beginner issues. Many projects tag issues for newcomers. Read the contributing guide first, then comment that you are working on it.
  4. Add tests or examples. Projects almost always welcome better tests and runnable examples.
  5. Share your own work. A well-documented package for a sensor or a robot that others use is a contribution by itself.

Be patient and polite. Reviews take time, and feedback is a gift. A single merged pull request in a well-known project is a strong line on a resume.

Interview preparation​

Robotics interviews usually mix several kinds of questions. Prepare for each.

  • Coding. Standard data structures and algorithms in Python or C++, since many roles ask for them. Practice writing clean code under time pressure.
  • Math and fundamentals. Rotation matrices and quaternions, composing transforms, forward and inverse kinematics, Bayes rule and a Kalman filter step, PID tuning, the Jacobian. Revisit Frames and rigid transforms and Probability for robots.
  • System design. "Design a pick-and-place system for a warehouse" or "How would you add voice control to a mobile robot?" Practice drawing blocks, naming interfaces, discussing failure modes and how you would test.
  • Debugging scenarios. "The robot drifts left. Walk me through your investigation." Your checklist from the build guide is the answer pattern: start from the lowest layer you can check and work upward.
  • Project deep dive. Be ready to explain every decision in your capstone, including what you would change. This is often the longest part.
  • Behavioral questions. A time you were stuck, a time you disagreed, a time something broke in front of other people. Prepare real stories with a clear outcome.
Practice out loud

Explain a technical topic from this course to a friend or a rubber duck in five minutes, without notes. The spots where you stumble are the spots to study.

A 90-day plan after the course​

Momentum matters. Without a plan, the weeks after a course tend to evaporate. Here is a plan; adjust the pace to your life.

Days 1 to 30: polish and publish.

  • Finish the capstone README, results and demo video.
  • Write one technical write-up and publish it on a blog or your repository.
  • Fix gaps found in the skills matrix by reviewing the relevant lessons.
  • Set up a profile page that links to your three best projects.

Days 31 to 60: go deep and go public.

  • Start the depth project in your chosen area, with a measurable goal.
  • Make your first open-source contribution (documentation or a small fix).
  • Join a robotics community, a local group or an online forum, and answer one question per week.
  • Practice interview fundamentals for 30 minutes a day.

Days 61 to 90: apply and iterate.

  • Finish the depth project and write it up.
  • Apply to roles, internships, research groups or open-source programs. Tailor each application to the role family it targets.
  • Do mock interviews with a friend and review your project deep dive.
  • Review progress: what worked, what did not, and plan the next 90 days.

Hands-on lab​

Build your portfolio page and your first contribution.

  1. Open your capstone repository and rewrite the README using the skeleton in this lesson. Include real results from your evaluation log.
  2. Record a three-minute demo with one honest failure, add captions and link it from the README.
  3. Create a skills matrix for yourself: copy the table, mark your current level for the role you want and list three gaps.
  4. Find one open-source robotics repository you used in the capstone, read its contributing guide and find one documentation or beginner-labelled issue you can address.
  5. Write your 90-day plan with dates and one measurable deliverable per month.

You know it worked when a stranger can open your README, understand the project in under two minutes and see real results.

Exercises​

Write the resume bulletBeginner

Turn this into a strong resume bullet: "Built a robot that fetches things when you talk to it." Use a verb, the technologies and a measured result.

Show solutionHide solution

Example: "Built a voice-commanded mobile manipulator in Gazebo Harmonic using ROS 2 Jazzy, Nav2 and MoveIt 2 with an LLM task planner and plan validator; achieved a 75 percent end-to-end success rate over 20 randomized trials and cut grasp failures by half by recalibrating camera extrinsics." Replace the numbers with your own measured ones.

Answer the debugging questionIntermediate

An interviewer asks: "Your mobile robot arrives 30 cm from the target on every run." Describe how you would investigate in five steps.

Show solutionHide solution

A good answer is systematic: (1) note that the error is consistent, which suggests a systematic cause, not noise; (2) check the frames: is the goal in the right frame and is the map frame aligned with where the robot thinks it is; (3) check localization quality and odometry calibration (wheel radius, track width); (4) test with a simple scripted motion to separate planning from execution; (5) verify the goal tolerance parameters, since a loose tolerance can stop the robot early. State what you would measure at each step.

Choose your laneBeginner

Pick the role you would most like in a year. List the three rows in the skills matrix where you are weakest for that role and one project that would fix each.

Show solutionHide solution

Answers will vary. A strong answer picks a role, names three honest gaps from its column (for example "Core: control theory"), and ties each to a small, concrete project with a deliverable, such as "implement and tune a joint-space PID on the simulated arm and plot step responses".

Takeaways

  • Robotics roles include software, perception, controls, robot learning, simulation and field engineering. Be deep in one and broad across the rest.
  • Use the skills matrix to find and close gaps for your chosen role.
  • A portfolio of three to five finished projects with real results beats many half-done ones.
  • Document honestly: demo video, results table, limitations.
  • Contribute to open source starting with documentation, bug reports and small fixes.
  • Prepare for coding, fundamentals, system design, debugging and project deep-dive questions, and follow a 90-day plan.
