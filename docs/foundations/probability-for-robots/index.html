Probability for robots

Beginner45 minBefore this:Python for robotics · Linear algebra and transforms

A distance sensor says the wall is 2.10 m away. A second sensor says 1.96 m. Neither is lying: both are noisy. Which one do you believe, and what should the robot conclude? Every sensor in robotics is wrong by some amount, so a robot has to reason about how wrong, and combine evidence sensibly. That reasoning is probability, and this lesson turns it into code you can run.

You will be able to

  • Describe a noisy measurement as a random variable with a mean and a variance.
  • Simulate Gaussian sensor noise and show that averaging reduces it by the square root of N.
  • Fuse two Gaussian measurements into one better estimate.
  • Apply Bayes rule to a discrete detector and to a robot localizing itself in a corridor.
  • Read a covariance matrix.

Noise, and a way to talk about it​

Point a range sensor at a wall exactly 2.00 m away and read it a hundred times. You will not get 2.00 each time. You get a cloud of values clustered around the truth. We describe this with a random variableA random variable is a quantity whose value varies from measurement to measurement, described by a probability distribution..

Two numbers summarise it:

  • The mean μ\muμ is the centre: the average value.
  • The variance σ2\sigma^2σ2 is the spread, and its square root σ\sigmaσ is the standard deviation, which has the same units as the measurement.

The most common model for sensor noise is the Gaussian (normal) distribution, written N(μ,σ2)\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)N(μ,σ2), with density

p(x)=12πσ2exp⁡ ⁣(−(x−μ)22σ2).p(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right).p(x)=2πσ2​1​exp(−2σ2(x−μ)2​).

For a Gaussian, about 68% of readings fall within one σ\sigmaσ of the mean, 95% within two, and 99.7% within three. Why Gaussian? Many small independent error sources add up, and the sum of many small random effects tends toward a Gaussian. It is also mathematically friendly, as you will see in the fusion step.

noise.py
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)          # fixed seed for repeatable results
true_range = 2.00                        # metres
sigma = 0.05                             # sensor standard deviation, metres

readings = true_range + rng.normal(0.0, sigma, size=100)
print(readings.mean())                   # close to 2.00
print(readings.std(ddof=1))              # close to 0.05

# Average N readings. How much does the average wobble?
for n in (1, 4, 16, 64, 256):
    means = (true_range + rng.normal(0.0, sigma, size=(5000, n))).mean(axis=1)
    print(n, means.std().round(4), (sigma / np.sqrt(n)).round(4))

The two printed columns match. The spread of an average of NNN readings is σ/N\sigma/\sqrt{N}σ/N​. Four times as many readings cut the noise in half. That is why robots average, and also why there is a limit: to be ten times more precise you need a hundred times the readings.

Noise is not the same as bias

Averaging removes random noise but not bias, a consistent error such as a sensor mounted slightly crooked. If every reading is 3 cm too long, a million readings will confidently tell you the wrong answer. Averaging cures noise, calibration cures bias.

Fusing two measurements​

Back to the two sensors. Sensor A says 2.10 m with σA=0.05\sigma_A=0.05σA​=0.05. Sensor B says 1.96 m with σB=0.02\sigma_B=0.02σB​=0.02. Sensor B is more precise, so it should count for more. The optimal combination of two independent Gaussian measurements weights each by the inverse of its variance:

1σ2=1σA2+1σB2,μ=σ2(μAσA2+μBσB2).\frac{1}{\sigma^2}=\frac{1}{\sigma_A^2}+\frac{1}{\sigma_B^2},\qquad \mu=\sigma^2\left(\frac{\mu_A}{\sigma_A^2}+\frac{\mu_B}{\sigma_B^2}\right).σ21​=σA2​1​+σB2​1​,μ=σ2(σA2​μA​​+σB2​μB​​).

Two things fall out. The fused mean lies between the two readings, closer to the more certain one. And the fused variance is smaller than either input, so combining sensors always helps, even if one is poor.

fuse.py
import numpy as np


def fuse(mu1, var1, mu2, var2):
    """Combine two independent Gaussian estimates of the same quantity."""
    var = 1.0 / (1.0 / var1 + 1.0 / var2)
    mu = var * (mu1 / var1 + mu2 / var2)
    return mu, var


mu, var = fuse(2.10, 0.05 ** 2, 1.96, 0.02 ** 2)
print(round(mu, 3), round(float(np.sqrt(var)), 4))   # 1.979  0.0186

The result is 1.979 m with a standard deviation of 1.9 cm: better than the best sensor alone, at 2 cm. This exact formula is the heart of the Kalman filter in Module 05. Keep it in mind.

Covariance: noise in more than one dimension​

A 2D position has an x error and a y error, and they may be linked. For example, a camera usually measures depth less accurately than sideways position, and the errors can tilt along a direction. The covariance matrix records this:

Σ=[σx2σxyσxyσy2].\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_x^2 & \sigma_{xy}\\ \sigma_{xy} & \sigma_y^2\end{bmatrix}.Σ=[σx2​σxy​​σxy​σy2​​].

The diagonal holds the variances. The off-diagonal σxy\sigma_{xy}σxy​ says whether the errors move together: positive means when x is too high, y tends to be too high as well.

covariance.py
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
cov = np.array([[0.04, 0.03],
                [0.03, 0.09]])
xy = rng.multivariate_normal([1.0, 2.0], cov, size=10_000)   # shape (10000, 2)
print(np.cov(xy.T).round(3))     # close to the matrix we started with

np.cov expects variables in rows, so we transpose. You will meet this matrix again as the uncertainty ellipse around a robot's estimated position.

Bayes rule: updating what you believe​

Suppose an obstacle detector has a hit rate of 90%: if an obstacle is there, it fires 90% of the time. It also has a false alarm rate of 20%: if nothing is there, it still fires 20% of the time. Obstacles are rare, present on 10% of the path. The detector fires. How likely is it that an obstacle is really there?

Most people say 90%. The right answer is lower. Bayes rule gives it:

P(obstacle∣alarm)=P(alarm∣obstacle) P(obstacle)P(alarm).P(\text{obstacle}\mid\text{alarm})=\frac{P(\text{alarm}\mid\text{obstacle})\,P(\text{obstacle})}{P(\text{alarm})}.P(obstacle∣alarm)=P(alarm)P(alarm∣obstacle)P(obstacle)​.

The numerator is 0.9×0.1=0.090.9\times0.1=0.090.9×0.1=0.09. The denominator adds both ways to get an alarm: 0.09+0.2×0.9=0.270.09+0.2\times0.9=0.270.09+0.2×0.9=0.27. So P=0.09/0.27≈0.33P=0.09/0.27\approx0.33P=0.09/0.27≈0.33. Only one alarm in three is real. The prior probability (obstacles are rare) matters a lot.

In words: posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior. You start with a belief (the prior), look at what the sensor says and how likely that reading would be under each possibility (the likelihood), multiply, and renormalise so everything sums to 1.

Belief is a probability distribution

A robot never knows where it is. It holds a belief: a probability for every place it might be. Sensing multiplies the belief by how well each place explains the reading. Moving shifts and blurs it, because motion is imperfect. Sense, move, sense, move: that is the loop of nearly all robot localization.

A robot in a corridor​

You will now implement that loop. A robot sits in a circular corridor of 8 cells with doors in cells 0 and 3, and walls elsewhere. It does not know which cell it is in, so it starts with a uniform belief. This is a histogram filter.

histogram_filter.py
import numpy as np

world = np.array(["door", "wall", "wall", "door", "wall", "wall", "wall", "wall"])
belief = np.full(len(world), 1.0 / len(world))     # start: no idea


def sense(belief, z, p_hit=0.8, p_miss=0.2):
    """Bayes update. z is what the sensor reports: 'door' or 'wall'."""
    likelihood = np.where(world == z, p_hit, p_miss)
    posterior = belief * likelihood
    return posterior / posterior.sum()


def move(belief, steps, p_exact=0.8, p_under=0.1, p_over=0.1):
    """Shift the belief by steps cells, allowing for slip (undershoot / overshoot)."""
    return (p_exact * np.roll(belief, steps)
            + p_under * np.roll(belief, steps - 1)
            + p_over * np.roll(belief, steps + 1))


belief = sense(belief, "door")
print(belief.round(3))     # peaks at cells 0 and 3
belief = move(belief, 1)
belief = sense(belief, "wall")
belief = move(belief, 2)
belief = sense(belief, "door")
print(belief.round(3), belief.argmax())    # strongest peak at cell 3

Walk through it. The first reading "door" leaves two equally good candidates, cells 0 and 3, because the corridor looks the same at both. Moving and sensing a wall does not separate them. After moving two more cells and seeing "door" again, only one starting guess fits the story, and the belief piles up on cell 3 at about 0.49. The robot has localized itself from nothing but noisy readings and noisy motion.

Why it still has some doubt

The belief does not become 1.0. The sensor is wrong 20% of the time and the motion slips 20% of the time, so honest uncertainty remains. A good localizer keeps that doubt visible instead of hiding it.

Fuse three sensorsIntermediate

Write fuse_many(mus, variances) that combines any number of independent Gaussian estimates of one quantity, and test it with readings 2.0, 2.1 and 1.9 with equal variance 0.01.

Show solutionHide solution
import numpy as np

def fuse_many(mus, variances):
    mus = np.asarray(mus, dtype=float)
    variances = np.asarray(variances, dtype=float)
    weights = 1.0 / variances
    var = 1.0 / weights.sum()
    mu = var * (weights * mus).sum()
    return mu, var

print(fuse_many([2.0, 2.1, 1.9], [0.01, 0.01, 0.01]))   # (2.0, 0.00333...)

With equal variances the fused mean is the plain average, and the variance is one third of the original. The general rule: add the inverse variances, and the result is the inverse of the sum.

Takeaways

  • Sensor readings are random variables, usually modelled as Gaussians with a mean and a variance.
  • Averaging N readings reduces noise by the square root of N, but never removes bias.
  • Fusing independent Gaussians weights each one by its inverse variance and always reduces uncertainty.
  • A covariance matrix records variances and how errors move together.
  • Bayes rule says posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior, and priors matter for rare events.
  • Localization alternates sensing (multiply by likelihood) and moving (shift and blur).
