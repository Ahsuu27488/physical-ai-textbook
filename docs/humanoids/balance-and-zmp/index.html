Balance, the ZMP and the inverted pendulum

Intermediate40 minBefore this:Dynamics essentials · Control and PID · Python basics

Someone bumps a standing humanoid from behind. In the first tenth of a second the robot has already been pushed off balance, and what it does next decides whether it stays up. Move the feet? Lean? Swing the arms? To answer that, we need a model simple enough to reason about in real time and good enough to predict a fall. That model is a stick with a weight on top.

You will be able to

  • Explain the support polygon and the center of mass, and why a robot can be statically stable.
  • Define the zero moment point (ZMP) and say when it leaves the support polygon.
  • Write and solve the linear inverted pendulum model (LIPM).
  • Compute the capture point and use it to choose a recovery step.
  • Simulate a push and recovery in a short Python program.

Standing: the support polygon​

The center of massThe point where the total mass of the robot can be treated as concentrated. For balance we usually care about where it is, projected straight down onto the floor. (CoM) is the average position of all the robot’s mass. The support polygonThe smallest convex shape that contains all the points where the feet touch the ground. is the shape on the floor spanned by the contact points. With two feet planted, it covers both soles and the gap between them.

If the CoM projected onto the floor stays inside the support polygon and the robot moves slowly, it is statically stableStable without needing to move: gravity alone cannot tip it over.. A table works this way. But walking is not static: at some moments the CoM is outside the support polygon, and the robot is, briefly, falling forward and catching itself. We need a criterion that works for motion.

The zero moment point​

When a robot accelerates, the ground pushes back with a force that is spread over the sole of the foot. Add up that distributed force and you get one equivalent force acting at one point. The zero moment pointZero moment point: the point on the ground where the horizontal components of the total moment from gravity and inertia cancel. On flat ground with the feet in contact, it equals the center of pressure. (ZMP) is the point where the ground-reaction moments about the horizontal axes sum to zero.

The rule is short:

The ZMP must stay inside the support polygon

As long as the ZMP is inside the support polygon, the foot sits flat and the robot does not tip over its edge. When the ZMP reaches the edge, the foot starts to roll. The robot can no longer control its tilt with that foot, and it is falling unless something else (another step, a swung arm, a grab) takes over.

Think of standing on one foot and leaning. You push your toes down to stop yourself tipping forward. Your center of pressure moves toward your toes. When it hits the toe edge, you have run out of control authority and must step.

The linear inverted pendulum model​

A real humanoid has dozens of links. For balance, we compress it into three assumptions:

  1. All the mass sits at one point, the CoM.
  2. The CoM moves at a constant height zcz_czc​.
  3. The legs are massless; the only thing the ground does is push at the ZMP, which we call ppp.

Take moments about the ZMP in one horizontal direction xxx. Gravity tries to tip the mass over with moment mg(x−p)m g (x - p)mg(x−p), and the acceleration of the mass resists with mzcx¨m z_c \ddot{x}mzc​x¨. That is an inverted pendulum: left alone it falls exponentially, at rate ω\omegaω. For a leg-height of about 0.85 m, ω≈3.4 s−1\omega \approx 3.4\ \text{s}^{-1}ω≈3.4 s−1, so a small error doubles in roughly 0.2 s.

The model is linear because zcz_czc​ is fixed, which turns a hard nonlinear problem into one you can solve in closed form and optimize quickly. It ignores angular momentum, leg mass and height changes, and that is why real controllers add corrections on top.

Note

Everything above is written for one direction, xxx (forward). Sideways, yyy, follows the same equation with its own pyp_ypy​. In the LIPM the two directions are independent.

The capture point​

How hard a push can the robot absorb without stepping? Ask the opposite question: if the robot could put its foot (and therefore the ZMP) at any point ppp, where must it be so that the CoM comes to rest above it?

Define the capture pointThe point on the ground where the robot must place its foot so that the pendulum comes to a stop over it. (sometimes called the divergent component of motion):

ξ=x+x˙ω\xi = x + \frac{\dot{x}}{\omega}ξ=x+ωx˙​

Differentiate and use the LIPM equation:

ξ˙=x˙+x¨ω=ω(ξ−p)\dot{\xi} = \dot{x} + \frac{\ddot{x}}{\omega} = \omega (\xi - p)ξ˙​=x˙+ωx¨​=ω(ξ−p)

Two things follow. If you place the foot exactly at p=ξp = \xip=ξ, then ξ˙=0\dot{\xi} = 0ξ˙​=0: the capture point stops moving, and the CoM converges smoothly to rest above the foot. If ppp is anywhere else, ξ\xiξ diverges away from ppp exponentially. The CoM itself obeys x˙=ω(ξ−x)\dot{x} = \omega(\xi - x)x˙=ω(ξ−x), so it is pulled toward ξ\xiξ on its own. That makes ξ\xiξ the only part of the motion you actually have to control.

Step where you would otherwise fall

The capture point is where the robot will end up if it stops pushing. To stop, put the foot there. The distance from the current foot to the capture point is the step you need; if it is more than the longest step the legs can take, one step is not enough.

Simulating a push​

lipm_push.py
import math

G, ZC = 9.81, 0.85
W = math.sqrt(G / ZC)  # natural frequency, 1/s

def lipm(x0, v0, p, t):
    """Exact LIPM motion for a fixed ZMP p after t seconds."""
    c, s = math.cosh(W * t), math.sinh(W * t)
    x = (x0 - p) * c + (v0 / W) * s + p
    v = (x0 - p) * W * s + v0 * c
    return x, v

def capture_point(x, v):
    return x + v / W

def recover(x, v, foot, max_step=0.30, step_time=0.35, max_steps=6):
    """Step to the capture point each time, limited by leg reach."""
    for k in range(1, max_steps + 1):
        xi = capture_point(x, v)
        want = xi - foot
        step = max(-max_step, min(max_step, want))
        foot += step
        x, v = lipm(x, v, foot, step_time)
        print(f"  step {k}: foot={foot:6.3f} m  x={x:6.3f} m  v={v:6.3f} m/s")
        if abs(v) < 0.05 and abs(x - foot) < 0.05:
            return True
    return False

print("omega =", round(W, 3), "1/s")
for push in (0.3, 0.8, 1.6):
    print(f"push gives v = {push} m/s, capture point = {capture_point(0.0, push):.3f} m")
    print("  stays up:", recover(0.0, push, foot=0.0))

print("never step, v0 = 0.3 m/s:")
for t in (0.2, 0.4, 0.8):
    x, v = lipm(0.0, 0.3, 0.0, t)
    print(f"  t={t:.1f} s  x={x:6.3f} m  v={v:6.3f} m/s")

Run it. For the two smaller pushes the capture point lies within one step of the foot, so the robot places its foot there and the speed dies away over the next few updates (each printed line is one more step decision; when the foot is already at the capture point it simply stays). The 1.6 m/s push needs a step of 0.47 m, which is beyond the 0.30 m limit. The robot steps as far as it can, the capture point keeps running away from it, and recover reports failure. The last block shows what happens if the robot never steps: position and speed grow exponentially.

This is a toy: it places the foot instantly, ignores that stepping takes time during which the pendulum keeps falling, and assumes the foot can go anywhere. Real controllers add step timing, swing-leg dynamics and the option to shift the CoM or use the arms, but the core move (step to the capture point) is exactly what many humanoid push-recovery controllers do.

What if the ZMP is not enough?​

The LIPM assumes you control ppp freely inside the foot. When ppp hits the edge, you have other tools:

  • Ankle strategy: shift the ZMP within the foot. Good for small pushes.
  • Hip strategy: swing the upper body to create angular momentum and a restoring moment.
  • Stepping strategy: move the support polygon under the capture point. Best for large pushes.
  • Arm swing and grabbing: more angular momentum, or a new contact.

Humans blend them naturally. Robot controllers choose between them based on the size of the capture-point error.

Hands-on lab​

  1. Save lipm_push.py and run it. Record, for each push, whether the robot recovers and how many steps it takes.
  2. Change max_step to 0.15. Which of the three pushes can the robot still recover from?
  3. Change ZC to 1.2 (a taller robot). Does the natural frequency go up or down? Is the capture point for a 0.8 m/s push closer or further away?
  4. Set step_time=0.6 and see whether a slower step helps or hurts the 0.8 m/s push. Why?
  5. Done when you can predict, before running, whether a push of a given size will be recovered.

Takeaways

  • Static balance means the CoM projection stays inside the support polygon; walking breaks that rule on purpose.
  • The ZMP must stay inside the support polygon for the foot to stay flat.
  • The LIPM gives x¨=ω2(x−p)\ddot{x} = \omega^2 (x - p)x¨=ω2(x−p) with ω=g/zc\omega = \sqrt{g/z_c}ω=g/zc​​, and an exact solution with cosh and sinh.
  • The capture point ξ=x+x˙/ω\xi = x + \dot{x}/\omegaξ=x+x˙/ω is where to step to stop.
  • Beyond the ankle strategy there are hip, stepping and arm strategies; controllers choose by how big the error is.
  • Simple models are valuable because you can optimize over them in milliseconds.
